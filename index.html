Proposal for a new field of study

Posterity
Studies

The study of how to make good decisions on behalf of those who come after us: all future people, not yet born, who will inherit the consequences of what we choose today.

How should the present act well toward a future that cannot vote, cannot consent, and cannot hold us to account — and how do we build the institutions, incentives, and cultures that reliably make it so?

I

The problem of the absent future

Every mechanism a society trusts to produce good decisions works by giving the affected party a seat at the table.

Markets give them a price to pay. Elections give them a vote to cast. Contracts give them a signature to withhold, courts a suit to file, reputation a judgment to render. Each of these is a way for the people who will bear a decision's consequences to press back on the people making it. Future generations have none of them. They do not exist yet; they cannot bargain, cannot consent, cannot reciprocate, and cannot punish.

And yet almost everything that will matter most to them is being decided now — the composition of the atmosphere, the depth of the aquifer, the level of the debt, the safety of the technologies we deploy, the durability of the institutions they will be born into. This is the defining condition of intergenerational decision-making: we hold enormous power over people who hold no power over us. The ordinary machinery of accountability is silent in precisely the cases where the stakes run longest.

Posterity is the name for everyone on the far side of that asymmetry: all the people who will come after us, not only our own children and grandchildren, and not only those who will live in the next few decades. This field takes the whole of posterity as its horizon. It sets aside the language of stewardship, which casts the present as the custodian of a holding and whose care, in practice, tends to stop at one's own heirs and the near term. Deciding well for posterity means extending standing to people we will never meet, whoever and wherever they turn out to be.

II

Why this needs a field of its own

The obligation to the future is not a new idea. It is one of the oldest — and that is exactly the difficulty. It is old, it is everywhere, and it is scattered. Philosophers ask what we owe. Economists ask how to price it. Lawyers ask how to bind it. Psychologists ask why we ignore it. Ecologists measure what we are spending. Indigenous nations have governed by it for centuries. Each of these traditions has produced deep and rigorous knowledge about one face of the same problem — and each, working alone, sees only that face.

The wager of this proposal is that these contributions are additive, not redundant. Roughly twenty disciplines each hold a genuine, non-substitutable piece of the answer: the mechanism, the measurement, the instrument, the motive, the precedent, the limit. Assembled, they describe something none of them describes alone — not merely what we owe the future or how much it is worth, but the practical, designable question underneath both: given everything we know, how do we actually decide, and how do we build a society that decides well across generations? That question is normative, predictive, institutional, behavioral, and cultural at once. It has no academic home. This is the field proposed to house it.

It helps to be precise about what posterity studies is not, since it borrows from each of these and reduces to none:

  • Not intergenerational ethics — which asks what we owe the future, but stops at principle and rarely reaches the decision.
  • Not longtermism — which asserts that the far future matters enormously, but is a contested claim about value rather than a discipline of decision.
  • Not sustainability — which is largely bounded to the environmental and, in practice, often to the present; posterity studies extends the same care to institutions, knowledge, and culture.
  • Not futures studies — which forecasts and imagines, but neither prescribes how to choose nor holds anyone to account for the choice.

It draws on all of them and belongs to none. Its object is the decision itself, and the systems that produce it.

III

The disciplinary architecture

No honest diagram of this field is a set of boxes, because its disciplines do not partition — they overlap. Psychology touches motivation and institutions and transmission at once; the problem of discounting the future recurs, in different clothes, in psychology, economics, and philosophy alike. What follows is therefore not a taxonomy but a matrix of contributions: for each discipline, the scale at which it works, the methods it brings, the practical lever it offers, the goal it pursues, and the concepts it lends to a shared vocabulary.

Read down the first column and the field's shape appears. The disciplines arrange themselves along a single ladder of scale — from the body to the biosphere. That ladder, not a set of silos, is the anatomy of a decision made on behalf of posterity: who is deciding, on whose behalf, through what, and within what limits.

IV

What this project offers

A field is not only an argument; it is an engine. Once these disciplines share a name, a question, and a table, the project can generate what none of them produces alone — new knowledge, new methods, new funding, new people, and new tools for the decisions themselves. This is what it offers.

  • Research agenda

    A defining frontier of open problems

    A field is defined by its hard questions. This one opens an agenda no discipline currently owns — the non-identity problem, whose values should govern, the limits of representing the unborn, and how to value a distant future — giving researchers first-order questions with room to make their name.

  • Methods

    A shared, multi-epistemic toolkit

    A common method set — Future Design deliberation, intergenerational games, robust decision-making, comparative institutional analysis — that lets otherwise incompatible disciplines produce comparable, cumulative work instead of talking past one another.

  • Demonstrators

    Flagship studies only this field could produce

    Two or three concrete projects that prove the integration does real work — for instance, a full intergenerational reading of a water-conservation or aquifer decision through ethics, economics, hydrology, and law at once — turning the claim into evidence.

  • Infrastructure

    The apparatus to endure

    The scaffolding a field needs to persist: a journal or special issue, a working-paper series, a recurring convening, and a shared library of concepts and cases that later scholars can build on.

  • Funding

    New funding pathways

    Because the problem falls between existing review panels, it also falls between existing grants. A named field creates a fundable home and aligns squarely with major funder priorities — climate and resilience, sustainability, long-horizon and future-generations philanthropy, technological risk — and with the cross-directorate programs built for exactly this convergence.

  • Collaboration

    A cross-disciplinary network

    A standing consortium linking scholars across all twenty-one disciplines, and across institutions, who are working the same problem without knowing it — turning parallel effort into a shared, cumulative enterprise.

  • Training

    An interdisciplinary talent pipeline

    A path for people trained to think across these fields: a graduate certificate, doctoral and postdoctoral fellowships, and a summer school — producing both scholars and practitioners, from foresight analysts to future-generations officers.

  • Impact

    Translational tools for decision-makers

    Beyond scholarship, usable instruments — future-generations impact assessments, intergenerational accounting frameworks, decision protocols — for the governments, firms, communities, and Indigenous nations already facing these choices.

  • Public engagement

    A way to make the future felt

    Drawing on the arts and deliberative methods, the field can shift public imagination and build futures-literacy — helping citizens, students, and communities reason across generations rather than leaving the long view to specialists.

  • Institutional fit

    A mission a university can own

    For a host institution, the field advances existing commitments to global futures, sustainability, and use-inspired research, and gives them a distinctive, ownable identity — a reason to house it, not merely permit it.
