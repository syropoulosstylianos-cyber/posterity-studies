Building a new field for and of the future

Posterity
Studies

A new, interdisciplinary field that centers posterity, all future generations of humans and other species, as its animating object of study.

We have many of the pieces needed for a robust and practically useful understanding of posterity. What we lack is a common academic home for the hard, cross-cutting work of integrating them.

I

An inflection point

Humanity, and our planet, is at an inflection point.

While some measures of well-being are improving (childhood mortality, lifespan, access to basic necessities), other indicators of human and planetary thriving and sustainability are flashing red and trending downwards: biodiversity, land conversion, support for democracy, personal and collective meaning-making. A conflagration of major challenges and rapid societal changes, among them artificial intelligence, climate change, increasing zoonotic disease outbreaks, and decreasing social trust and cohesion, poses serious and growing threats to human and planetary flourishing in the decades to come. These challenges are already harming people and other species alive today. But there is widespread agreement that their worst impacts will accrue to future generations of humans and all species: that is, to posterity.

As the recently adopted United Nations Declaration on Future Generations makes clear, it is incumbent upon those of us alive today, acting both as individuals and collectively, to do the hard but necessary work of shifting course to promote a thriving future. All of society has a role to play in this work, and academics hold a special position. Through their dual role as scholars bringing new discoveries to light and educators who help sculpt the citizens of tomorrow, academics must be at the center of efforts to imagine and promote a thriving future. But to reach its full potential, something new, and currently lacking, must emerge: a more nuanced, interdisciplinary pursuit, one which centers posterity as its animating object of study.

II

Why a new field

Scholars have long asked questions about the relationship between present and future generations: what responsibilities we have towards future others, where care and concern for posterity come from, and how they break down when it comes to taking costly action. These are old questions, and the scholarship on the "problem of the future" is deep, rigorous, and diverse. Philosophers have long thought deeply about what present generations owe to future generations and what justice demands of us today. Psychologists, anthropologists, and sociologists are exploring how we think about, relate to, and act towards the future, and what motivates or inhibits stewardship at the individual level and across different cultures. Economists and political scientists have developed methods to show how much we truly value (or don't value) future generations through our actions and policies. Historians, theologians, policy analysts, planners, literary critics, artists, futurists, and many others have delved into the complex knot of factors that shape how we imagine and plan for future others, or fail to adequately do so.

Yet at present, scholarly work on posterity, and on the future more broadly, is scattered across a dozen or more disciplines that rarely interact with one another. A broad scan of the existing literatures on posterity has revealed to us just how siloed this scholarship is: the vast majority of extant work is both produced and disseminated within narrow disciplinary fields that largely remain ignorant of one another. This lack of communication and integration holds back our ability to think expansively, systematically, and creatively about posterity, about the challenges facing future generations of life on our planet, and about how present generations can act with the humility and foresight necessary to promote future flourishing. Conversely, bringing these distinct lines of inquiry and insight into direct dialogue and integrated practice holds the potential to transform how we understand, engage with, and address the challenges that posterity poses to the present: ethical, emotional, economic, political, and otherwise.

This, then, is the core justification for a new, interdisciplinary field of study: while we have many of the individual pieces needed to develop a robust and practically useful understanding of posterity, we lack a common academic home that can serve as the site for encouraging and supporting the challenging, cross-cutting work of integrating these diverse approaches to produce truly transdisciplinary insights. We call this new field, this new home, Posterity Studies.

It helps to be precise about what Posterity Studies is not, since it borrows from each of these and reduces to none:

  • Not intergenerational ethics It asks what we owe the future, one face of the problem, and leaves aside how people and institutions actually imagine, feel about, and act for the future.
  • Not longtermism It is one position on how much the far future matters. Posterity Studies examines such positions rather than adopting one.
  • Not sustainability It is largely bounded to the environmental and, in practice, often to the present. Posterity Studies extends the same care to institutions, knowledge, and culture.
  • Not futures studies It forecasts and imagines possible futures. Posterity Studies centers the people and other living things who will inhabit them.

It draws on all of them and belongs to none. Its object is posterity itself: how present generations understand, imagine, value, and act on behalf of those who come after us.

III

The disciplinary architecture

Scholarship on posterity is scattered across a dozen or more disciplines, and no honest diagram of them is a set of boxes, because they do not partition: they overlap. The field is therefore mapped not as a taxonomy but as a matrix of contributions, given in full at the end of this page: for each discipline, the scale at which it works, the methods it brings, the practical lever it offers, the goal it pursues, and the concepts it lends to a shared vocabulary.

Read down its scope column and the field's shape appears. The disciplines arrange themselves along a single ladder of scale — from the body to the biosphere. That ladder, not a set of silos, is the anatomy of a decision made on behalf of posterity: who is deciding, on whose behalf, through what, and within what limits.

IV

What the field needs, and where to begin

A new field needs many things to launch: shared theoretical and methodological frameworks, institutional backing and recognition, funding sources to support new research, and a shared set of overarching research questions and challenges that animate the work, to name just a few. But at the beginning of such an endeavour, perhaps the three most important supports are these.

  • Community A robust and diverse community of open-minded, intellectually humble scholar-educators who are excited about and dedicated to the work.
  • Core texts A set of co-developed, shared core texts that gather and begin to integrate extant scholarship in cohesive, mutually legible collections.
  • Curricular vision A vision for undergraduate and graduate curricula that lays out what it would mean to train students in the field of Posterity Studies.

We believe developing these three foundational components can jumpstart the new field and provide an anchor for those who wish to engage with and support the broader project. To that end, we envision pursuing a wide range of possible activities to develop these anchors and rapidly establish and grow the field. In the early years of this effort, these activities could include, but are not limited to, the following.

Activities in the early years

  • Community

    A cohort of scholars of posterity

    A highly engaged cohort drawn from a diverse set of fields, meeting repeatedly in small groups to build community, produce early shared texts (special issues in key journals, a handbook of posterity scholarship), and co-develop educational products (course syllabi, draft curricula).

  • Convening

    International conferences

    One or more large, international academic conferences to further grow, democratize, and diversify the field.

  • Institutions

    University-based institutes or centers

    One or more new institutes or centers focused on research, education, and outreach or practice.

  • Funding

    New funding and re-granting opportunities

    Support for scholarship and training in Posterity Studies, including possible graduate student and postdoctoral fellowships.

  • Publishing

    A journal of Posterity Studies

    A new interdisciplinary journal dedicated to the field.

  • Network

    Scholars and practitioners together

    A network of scholars and practitioners working within the broad domain of Posterity Studies, to promote integration and cross-sector learning.

V

Why now

We believe the current moment is unusually ripe for developing a new field of Posterity Studies. As we enter a time of incredibly rapid technological and societal transformation, the future looms particularly large in the minds of many. At the individual level, many people are wondering, often with significant trepidation, what the future holds for themselves, their communities, and especially their children and grandchildren.

At the institutional and societal level, we are seeing significant movement towards taking our responsibilities towards posterity more seriously than ever before. Wales has legislated a Future Generations Commissioner; the United Nations' Pact for the Future (2024) adopted a Declaration on Future Generations; and constitutional courts have begun giving legal standing to the unborn.

Practice is running ahead of theory. Posterity Studies is the field that can help guide what comes next.
